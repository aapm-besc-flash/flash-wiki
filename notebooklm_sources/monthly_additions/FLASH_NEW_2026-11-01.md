## 1. FLASH reduces radiation-induced oral mucositis in a mouse model of Fanconi anemia.
Authors: Loo P, Pan M, Zhao M, Melemenidis S, Schulz JB, Chen D, Whitmore L, Richter S et al.
Journal: Clinical and translational radiation oncology (2027)
Identifiers: PMID 42964716; DOI 10.1016/j.ctro.2026.101334; PMC PMC13721873 (open access)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42964716/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Radiobiology

Abstract: This study demonstrates that FLASH radiotherapy reduces oral mucositis in both wild-type and Fanca-deficient mice. At the cellular level, FLASH attenuated radiation-induced inflammation in the tongue relative to conventional irradiation in both genotypes, indicating that FLASH mitigates inflammatory processes associated with oral mucositis. Collectively, these findings support the potential of FLASH radiotherapy to reduce radiation-induced oral mucositis in the context of Fanca deficiency and the Fanconi anemia genomic background.

## 2. VHEE beam parameter extraction from depth-dose and lateral profile measurements: a Monte Carlo parametric study.
Authors: Rezzoug M, Oulhouq Y, Hamzaoui O, Zerfaoui M, Rrhioua A, Bakari D
Journal: Radiological physics and technology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42965639; DOI 10.1007/s12194-026-01160-9
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42965639/
Category: Physics & Dosimetry | Tags: Beam Delivery & Technology, Modeling & Mechanisms, Physics & Dosimetry

Abstract: Very High Energy Electrons (VHEE, 50-300 MeV) are a promising modality for deep-seated tumour irradiation. Accurate Monte Carlo (MC) dose calculation requires precise determination of five primary beam parameters: Energy (E), Source size (σ₀), angular divergence (θ), energy spread (ΔE), and source-to-surface distance (SSD). This study quantified the independent influence of each parameter and derived inverse formulae enabling their reconstruction from measured percentage depth-dose (PDD) curves and lateral dose profiles. GATE v9.3 simulations were performed for 50-300 MeV electron beams in a homogeneous water phantom (30 × 30 × 30 cm³, 1 mm³ voxels). A one-factor-at-a-time approach varied E, σ₀ (0-10 mm), θ (0-80 mrad), ΔE (0-50 MeV), and SSD (0-100 cm). For each configuration, 2 × 10⁹ primary electrons were simulated, yielding uncertainties < 1% at Dmax and < 3% distally. Longitudinal observables (R100, R90, R80, R50, R20, surface dose, distal width W=R20 - R80) and lateral observables (FWHM, penumbra) were extracted. Inverse relationships - relating beam energy to R50, source size to FWHM, divergence to R90, and energy spread to the distal width W - were derived by weighted least-squares regression - expressing beam energy as a function of R50, source size as a function of the lateral field size (FWHM), divergence as a function of R90, and energy spread as a function of the distal fall-off width (W = R20 - R80) - and validated using five independent datasets. Longitudinal observables followed a quadratic model in σ₀ (adjusted R²>0.98, RMSE < 2 mm). For the reference configuration, energy was related to R50 by E=(R50 - 38.97)/0.5699. Source size was obtained from σ₀=FWHM/2.355, divergence from R90, and energy spread from W. Validation showed energy agreement within ± 4% for five datasets. The proposed inverse formulae enable rapid and accurate extraction of VHEE beam parameters from routine dosimetric measurements, supporting beam commissioning, quality assurance, and TPS validation for VHEE and FLASH-VHEE systems.

## 3. Analysis on radiation protection of FLASH treatment in conventional proton therapy facilities.
Authors: Wang Y, Deng C, Gao N, Zhang Y, Wang Y, Liu M, Zhou T, Lu YM et al.
Journal: Medical physics (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42961338; DOI 10.1002/mp.70747
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42961338/
Category: Physics & Dosimetry | Tags: Radiobiology, Physics & Dosimetry, Beam Delivery & Technology, Clinical & Translational

Abstract: BACKGROUND: FLASH radiotherapy is an advanced radiotherapy technique featuring ultra-high dose-rate irradiation. Previous preclinical studies have shown that it can significantly reduce damage to normal tissues while preserving the ability to kill tumor cells. Due to the very high current intensity of FLASH radiotherapy, the effectiveness of radiation shielding in current proton therapy facilities needs to be evaluated prior to clinical studies. PURPOSE: Based on the existing shielding conditions of a ProBeam proton therapy system for conventional proton therapy treatment, we studied the shielding effectiveness of FLASH treatment in both the transmission and Bragg-peak modes. METHODS: Under the conditions of single-point irradiation using a proton beam with the highest clinical energy of 244MeV, and the beam intensity of 5nA, 10nA, and 20nA at the nozzle, the ambient equivalent dose rate outside the shielding door was measured with neutron and γ detector, in order to determine the linear relationship between the current intensity and the ambient dose rate. In addition, a gantry room was used as the study object, and the layout design of the points of interest was carried out according to the layout of the proton treatment facility and the transport path and direction of the proton beam, and the most unfavorable working conditions were set for each point of interest. The beam energy was set to 244 MeV, with a cylindrical water phantom (20 cm in diameter) and a solid water block (40 × 40 × 40 cm3) placed at the isocenter. The current at the nozzle was set to 20 nA, and comprehensive environmental dose rate measurements were carried out for the proton radiation workplace. RESULTS: A linear relationship was observed between nozzle current and ambient dose equivalent rate. Based on the linear relationship, when the current was extrapolated to a FLASH current of 170 nA, the measured instantaneous dose rates at some locations were relatively high, with the maximum value of 74.04 µSv/h recorded outside the shielding door. Under the conservative assumption that the total annual beam delivery time is 5 h (treating 6 patients per hour), the maximum annual occupational exposure doses for radiation workers under the transmission and Bragg Peak modes are 0.370 mSv and 0.236 mSv, respectively, which are well below the annual occupational dose limit. CONCLUSIONS: The results indicate that, under existing conventional shielding conditions, the radiation risk posed to the environment outside the treatment room by FLASH irradiation using the Varian ProBeam proton therapy system is safe and controllable, and the annual occupational radiation dose for radiation workers is well below the annual dose equivalent limit. This provides important radiation safety justification for the clinical implementation of FLASH technology in existing proton therapy facilities.

## 4. Experimental validation of time-driven proton PBS FLASH delivery on a clinical proton therapy system.
Authors: Krzempek D, Gajewski J, Jabłoński H, Spaleniak A, Komenda W, Guguła K, Kamiński M, Hankiewicz M et al.
Journal: Physics and imaging in radiation oncology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42959518; DOI 10.1016/j.phro.2026.101110; PMC PMC13717095 (open access)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42959518/
Category: Beam Delivery & Technology | Tags: Beam Delivery & Technology, Physics & Dosimetry, Radiobiology

Abstract: BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Clinical proton pencil beam scanning systems use dose-driven delivery, limiting operation at ultra-high dose rates required for FLASH radiotherapy. Time-driven spot delivery, controlled by irradiation time rather than monitor chamber feedback, may enable FLASH operation. We implemented a non-clinical time-driven FLASH strategy on a clinical proton PBS system and experimentally validated its performance. MATERIALS AND METHODS: FLASH delivery was achieved by increasing beamline transmission through the energy-selection system and replacing dose-driven spot control with time-driven delivery in the Scanning Controller Diagnostic mode. Beam transmission was evaluated at gantry angle 0°. Depth-dose distributions, beam optics, scanning accuracy and uniform field generation were assessed for 228.4 MeV protons at gantry angles of 0°, 180° and 270°. RESULTS: Opening the ESS divergence-limiting slits increased beamline transmission from 8% to 57%, resulting in approximately 273 nA at the isocentre (1.7 × 1012 protons s-1) and an estimated peak on-axis average dose rate of 2.0 × 103 Gy s-1. An 11 × 11 spot field delivered 25 Gy in approximately 0.6 s, corresponding to an average field dose rate of 42 Gy s-1, with up to 90 Gy s-1 measured at higher doses. Proton range and Bragg peak width remained unchanged. Spot position deviations were below 0.5 mm, and uniform 4 × 4 cm2 fields with <3% flatness were achieved. CONCLUSIONS: Time-driven delivery enables experimental proton PBS operation at UHDR while maintaining beam characteristics suitable for reproducible dosimetry and radiobiological studies, providing a practical and experimentally characterised platform for preclinical proton PBS FLASH research.

## 5. Differences in Biological Responses to Ultra-high Dose Rate Proton Irradiation Between Entrance and SOBP Regions In Vitro.
Authors: Nomura S, Hasegawa M, Inoue J, Inoue K, Yoshimura H, Isohashi F, Mori E
Journal: Anticancer research (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42946440; DOI 10.21873/anticanres.18439
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42946440/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Radiobiology, Beam Delivery & Technology

Abstract: BACKGROUND/AIM: Ultra-high dose rate (FLASH) radiotherapy has been reported to reduce normal tissue toxicity while maintaining tumor control. However, whether proton FLASH induces different biological responses in the entrance versus spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) regions under controlled irradiation conditions remains unclear. This study compared biological responses to ultra-high dose rate proton irradiation in the entrance and SOBP regions at matched dose and time-averaged dose rate conditions. MATERIALS AND METHODS: Non-tumor V79 Chinese hamster lung fibroblasts cells were irradiated with 230 MeV protons using pencil beam scanning under hypoxic (1% O2) conditions. Dose-rate conditions of 1, 40, and 100 Gy/s were applied for entrance irradiation, and 1 and 100 Gy/s for SOBP irradiation. The surviving fraction was analyzed using a clonogenic cell survival assay. RESULTS: Clonogenic survival decreased dose-dependently at all dose rates. In the entrance region, no consistent dose-rate differences were observed up to 21 Gy; at 24 Gy, 100 Gy/s yielded a significantly higher surviving fraction than 1 Gy/s. In the SOBP region, the surviving fraction did not differ significantly between 1 and 100 Gy/s at 24 Gy. CONCLUSION: Under controlled conditions, a significant dose-rate-associated difference in clonogenic survival was observed in the entrance region at 24 Gy but not in the SOBP region. Further studies are required to determine whether the dose rate effect differs according to irradiation depth.

## 6. Scintillating Micro-Probe Detectors for Conventional and FLASH Proton Dosimetry: Optical Transport and Geometry Modeling.
Authors: Subedi SK, Debnath SBC
Journal: Bioengineering (Basel, Switzerland) (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42939044; DOI 10.3390/bioengineering13101189; PMC PMC13701824 (open access)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42939044/
Category: Physics & Dosimetry | Tags: Physics & Dosimetry, Modeling & Mechanisms

Abstract: Optical fiber-based inorganic scintillating detectors (ISDs) are increasingly investigated for radiation therapy applications, including microbeam and ultra-high-dose-rate dosimetry. Accurate characterization of these compact detectors is particularly important for proton beams delivered under high-flux and FLASH conditions. Simplified analytical and ray-tracing models can estimate detector saturation limits but may inadequately account for internal optical scattering and the fiber insertion geometry within the scintillating volume, leading to inaccurate estimates of the effective sensitive volume and dose-rate response. In this study, we developed a detailed computational framework that explicitly models the fiber-scintillator geometry and volumetric optical photon transport. Five detector geometries with different sensitive volumes were evaluated under 25 and 230 MeV proton irradiation using a fully volumetric GATE Monte Carlo optical transport model incorporating intra-dosimeter optical scattering. The results revealed a clear inverse relationship between detector sensitive volume and maximum measurable dose rate before saturation. At 25 MeV, the maximum measurable dose rate (D˙max) ranged from 364.5 ± 7.6 Gy/s for the smallest detector to 13.5 ± 0.2 Gy/s for the largest detector, while at 230 MeV, it ranged from 392.6 ± 8.1 Gy/s down to 15.6 ± 0.3 Gy/s at 230 MeV, respectively. These findings demonstrate that fiber insertion geometry and volumetric optical scattering significantly influence the dose-rate response and saturation threshold of scintillating micro-probes. Accurate modeling of these effects is therefore essential for optimizing detector geometry, establishing reliable quantitative dosimetry across conventional and proton-FLASH dose-rate regimes.

## 8. Charge collection efficiency in air-vented plane-parallel ionisation chambers at ultra-high dose rates: a self-consistent Garfield++ Monte Carlo model including space-charge effects and ion recombination.
Authors: Gérard Ortega P, De Lentdecker G, Flandroy Q, Nys J
Journal: Physics in medicine and biology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42933125; DOI 10.1088/1361-6560/aeb99e
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42933125/
Category: Physics & Dosimetry | Tags: Physics & Dosimetry, Modeling & Mechanisms

Abstract: Ultra-high dose rate (UHDR) irradiation used in FLASH radiotherapy can lead to significant reductions in charge collection efficiency (CCE) in plane-parallel ionisation chambers (PPICs) due to enhanced ion recombination, while the high charge densities involved can also induce strong space-charge-induced electric-field distortions. This work aims to develop a Monte Carlo framework for investigating these coupled effects.&#xD;&#xD;Approach: We extended the Garfield++ Monte Carlo particle-transport framework by implementing ion-ion recombination and self-consistent space-charge electric field calculations. The resulting model couples particle transport, electron attachment, recombination processes, and dynamic electric-field distortions. The implementation was validated against analytical and numerical models from the literature.&#xD;&#xD;Main results: Excellent agreement was obtained for the free electron fraction (FEF), CCE, induced current, and electric field evolution under UHDR conditions. The simulations show that space charge can locally increase the electric field by more than a factor of four or reduce it to nearly zero in some regions of the chamber. The results further suggest that the decrease in the FEF caused by electric-field-dependent electron attachment plays a major role in the reduction of the CCE under UHDR conditions.&#xD;&#xD;Significance: These findings highlight the importance of accurately describing the evolution of the FEF for modelling recombination under UHDR conditions, opening up prospects for improved analytical models and real-time correction methods for ionisation chamber dosimetry. More broadly, this work provides a flexible and self-consistent Monte Carlo framework for investigating recombination phenomena and improving dosimetry models for FLASH radiotherapy.

## 9. Mind the data: pitfalls and perspectives in acquiring and interpreting preclinical evidence for the FLASH effect.
Authors: Böhlen TT, Sesink A, Adrian G, Beyreuther E, Desmet A, Grilj V, Guckenberger M, Montay-Gruel P et al.
Journal: Radiotherapy and oncology : journal of the European Society for Therapeutic Radiology and Oncology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42925959; DOI 10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111819
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42925959/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Radiobiology, Physics & Dosimetry, Clinical & Translational

Abstract: FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH-RT) has rapidly emerged as a promising approach to widen the therapeutic window through differential sparing of normal tissue. However, despite substantial experimental activity, uncertainties remain regarding the magnitude, reproducibility, and determinants of the FLASH effect. This work reviews common pitfalls and sources of ambiguity in the acquisition and interpretation of preclinical FLASH data to support clinical application. In particular, the widespread use of fixed single-dose study designs, over-reliance on differential endpoint-based metrics, and limited quantification of effect size may obscure the true magnitude and clinical relevance of observed effects. Moreover, small dose-modifying effects are highly susceptible to experimental and biological variability, including dosimetric uncertainties, endpoint sensitivity, and environmental factors such as oxygenation. Heterogeneity in experimental protocols and endpoints further complicates cross-study comparisons. Addressing these challenges through improved quantitative characterisation, harmonised methodologies, and transparent reporting will be essential to enable robust interpretation and support the clinical translation of FLASH-RT.

## 10. Photon FLASH spares radiation-induced lung injury and pulmonary hypertension in a preclinical model.
Authors: Brown KH, Ghita-Pettigrew M, McCabe L, McDowell MP, Kerr BN, Walls GM, McMahon SJ, Butterworth KT
Journal: Radiotherapy and oncology : journal of the European Society for Therapeutic Radiology and Oncology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42925958; DOI 10.1016/j.radonc.2026.111822
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42925958/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Radiobiology

Abstract: INTRODUCTION: Ultra-high dose rate (UHDR) radiotherapy has shown significant promise in reducing normal tissue toxicities with equivalent tumour control compared to conventional (CONV) dose rates termed the FLASH effect. In this study, we compared radiation-induced lung injury and changes in the pulmonary circulation following localised right lung irradiation at UHDR and CONV exposures using photons. METHODS: Acute and late pulmonary radiation responses were assessed in pneumonitis (C3H/NeJ) and fibrosis prone (C57BL/6) mice. 18 Gy was delivered to the right lung at CONV (0.05 Gy/s), FLASH (76.2 Gy/s) and FLASH split dose (FSD, average 2.6 Gy/s) exposures. Early responses (72 h and 4 weeks after irradiation) were investigated by immunohistochemistry (Ly6G, F4/80), fluorescence staining (EdU) and serum profiling (cytokines and lipid peroxidation assays). Fibrosis was assessed at 30 weeks after irradiation by cone-beam CT (CBCT) analysis and quantification of collagen, senescence (p21) and TGF-β levels. Changes in pulmonary vasculature and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was assessed in C57BL/6 mice at 30 weeks to characterise changes in the pulmonary circulation associated with pulmonary hypertension (PH). RESULTS: At early timepoints, UHDR exposures led to reduced immune cell infiltration (neutrophils and macrophages) and tissue damage in comparison to CONV and FSD groups. Serum analysis supported FLASH sparing with decreased levels of markers associated with chronic inflammation and increased markers of tissue repair in comparison to CONV and FSD groups. At 30 weeks after irradiation at a UHDR, the lung parenchyma was structurally similar to controls (p = 0.33) with minimal collagen deposition supported by CBCT tissue density analysis. In contrast, CONV and FSD animals showed significant collagen deposition (p < 0.01) with thickened alveolar septa and reduced alveolar air spaces. UHDR exposures significantly reduced functional and histological metrics of PH (p < 0.05) compared to CONV. CONCLUSIONS: UHDR photons significantly reduce radiation-induced pneumonitis and fibrosis with secondary effects on the pulmonary circulation sparing PH.

## 11. Ultra-high-dose-rate pulsed irradiation and the conditional FLASH effect: dosimetry, multiscale biology, and clinical translation.
Authors: Zhang J, Lu H, Dong G, Wang C
Journal: Frontiers in oncology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42909922; DOI 10.3389/fonc.2026.1990706; PMC PMC13684859 (open access)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42909922/
Category: Reviews & Consensus | Tags: Reviews & Consensus, Radiobiology, Physics & Dosimetry, Clinical & Translational, Modeling & Mechanisms

Abstract: Ultra-high-dose-rate (UHDR) pulsed irradiation can deliver therapeutic doses within an extremely short time window, but UHDR is a physical delivery state, whereas the FLASH effect is a conditional comparative phenotype in which normal-tissue injury is reduced relative to a spatially dose-comparable conventional-dose-rate (CDR) irradiation comparator without loss of tumor control. This Review integrates evidence across four levels: physical exposure characterization, dosimetric measurement, biological comparison, and clinical translation. We examine the temporal structure and quality-assurance requirements of electron, very-high-energy electron, photon, proton, and heavy-ion UHDR platforms; synthesize positive, negative, and conditional findings across normal-tissue and tumor models; and integrate mechanistic studies spanning multiple time scales. Current evidence does not support 40 Gy/s, or any single average dose-rate metric, as either a sufficient or a necessary threshold for the FLASH effect. Effect magnitude and reproducibility are more likely determined jointly by dose per pulse, pulse structure, total delivery time, fractionation, irradiated volume, radiation quality, tissue-specific radiobiological characteristics, and biological state. Mechanistic data likewise favor multiscale coupling across time rather than a single explanatory pathway, although several causal links from early oxygen and radical dynamics to cellular and tissue outcomes still lack direct interventional validation. Human studies have established the feasibility of clinical UHDR delivery on selected electron and proton platforms and have provided preliminary safety data, but comparative clinical benefit over CDR irradiation has not yet been demonstrated. Further translation should therefore prioritize pulse-resolved traceable dosimetry, organ-specific normal-tissue dose-response relationships, late toxicity, comparable tumor control, and concurrent comparative clinical endpoints, with expansion guided by prespecified stage-gated translational criteria.

## 12. End-to-end film-based characterization of gated delivery latencies in a spot-scanned synchrotron-based proton therapy system.
Authors: Stottler ZS, Kruse JJ, Deisher AJ
Journal: Journal of applied clinical medical physics (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42905951; DOI 10.1002/acm2.70826; PMC PMC13686591 (open access)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42905951/
Category: Beam Delivery & Technology | Tags: Physics & Dosimetry, Beam Delivery & Technology

Abstract: BACKGROUND: Temporal mismatches between gating signals and beam activation-beam-on and beam-off latencies-in gated particle beam therapy can compromise dose integrity. Yet standardized measurement techniques are lacking, and system-specific characterization, particularly for synchrotron-based proton systems, remains limited. PURPOSE: To implement a novel strategy for precise end-to-end gating latency measurements of a spot-scanned proton beam using Gafchromic film. METHODS: Gating latencies have been measured for a Hitachi ProBeat accelerator equipped with a Varian RPM gating system. EBT4 films were positioned within a stack of acrylic blocks on a QUASAR respiratory motion platform operated with a 1.5 cm amplitude and 12 bpm sinusoidal pattern. A stainless-steel ball bearing (BB), positioned atop the film, facilitated localization. During irradiation, the BB produced a dose shadow on the film that indicated the phantom position. A proton plan consisting of a single 85.3 MeV spot was delivered to isocenter during a single gate-on/off interval initiated near the 25% phase, to maximize phantom velocity. Fifty-one measurements were made with a variety of gating intervals. Beam-on latency and delivery time produced a separation between the BB-shadow and the centroid of the Gaussian proton spot. Spot duration information was extracted from accelerator log files. BB-shadow/spot separation is modeled analytically based on phantom motion enabling the direct calculation of the total beam-on and beam-off latencies. Accelerator latency was measured directly with an oscilloscope, enabling isolation of the relative contributions of the RPM gating system. RESULTS: An average beam-on latency of 142 ± 27  ms is observed for the RPM+ProBeat system for the first gate of a delivery, which includes a known 69 ms beam extraction judgment time for the first proton spot in a spill from the Hitachi synchrotron. The corresponding average beam-off latency is 87 ± 30  ms  . The average gate-on and gate-off latencies of the RPM system are calculated as 72 ± 27  ms  and 81 ± 30  ms  , respectively. The associated average contributions of the accelerator to the beam-on latencies are measured as 69.3 ± 0.2  ms  (first gate in new spill) and 0.53 ± 0.07  ms  (subsequent gates in existing spill) and similarly 6 ± 4  ms  for beam-off latency. CONCLUSION: A novel, high-precision method for gating-latency measurements has been implemented using film to characterize gating latencies in a spot-scanned particle beam. Commissioning and regular quality assurance methods to precisely characterize the temporal accuracy of a gating system ensure treatment accuracy and will become increasingly vital to consider for higher-frequency applications, such as cardiac gating or FLASH-capable systems.

## 13. Impact of proton LET and ultrahigh dose-rate on the structural integrity of DNA in solution.
Authors: Adhikary A, Sforza DM, Yin L, Bunz F, Rezaee M
Journal: International journal of radiation biology (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42905122; DOI 10.1080/09553002.2026.2747690
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42905122/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Modeling & Mechanisms, Radiobiology, Physics & Dosimetry

Abstract: PURPOSE: Ultra-high dose-rate (UHDR) irradiation reduces normal tissue toxicity while maintaining tumor control under isodose conditions with respect to conventional dose-rate (CONV) irradiation (the FLASH effect). But its underlying physicochemical mechanism remains highly debated. This study evaluates whether radical-radical recombination can explain this sparing without invoking bulk oxygen depletion by decoupling the roles of track structure (linear energy transfer, LET) and particle fluence and velocity on free-radical kinetics and DNA damage. MATERIALS AND METHODS: Highly purified pUC19 plasmid DNA in aqueous oxygenated buffer was irradiated with 142.4 MeV protons at CONV (∼1.0 Gy/s) or UHDR (>455 Gy/s). Samples were positioned in a water phantom and received doses ranging from 0 to 160 Gy at LETd (dose-averaged LET) values of 1.7, 4.4, and 6.5 keV/µm. Strand breaks and formamidopyrimidine-DNA glycosylase (Fpg) and endonuclease III (Nth) -sensitive base lesions were quantified by gel electrophoresis. At low LET, fluence was estimated from Φ = ρD/LET and mean nearest-track distance from dNN = 1/(2√Φ), using the dose delivered during one OH• lifetime (approximately 0.91 µs). RESULTS: At LETd = 1.7 keV/µm, the estimated mean nearest-track distance during one OH• lifetime was ∼275 µm at 1.0 Gy/s and ∼13 µm at 455 Gy/s. Even the UHDR value was ∼100 times larger than the ∼0.1-0.13 µm OH• diffusion distance. Above ∼40 Gy cumulative dose, UHDR produced lower strand-break and enzyme-sensitive lesion values than CONV, and the CONV-UHDR difference decreased as LETd increased. CONCLUSIONS: Proton dose-rate alone is insufficient to predict the FLASH effect-mediated tissue sparing. Our results demonstrate that sparing is a track-structure-dependent kinetic phenomenon. LET, fluence, spill structure, solution chemistry, and oxygen history must be considered together. The data constrain candidate FLASH mechanisms but do not identify a unique chemical pathway. DEFINITIONS: Spur: a localized cluster of closely spaced ionizations and excitations.Branch: a lateral secondary-electron trajectory containing one or more additional spurs.Intra-track recombination: reactions among species generated within the same primary-particle track and its branches.Inter-track recombination: reactions between species generated by different primary-particle tracks. Track coincidence requires two tracks to be created close enough in both space and time for their reactive species to meet.Mean nearest-track distance: for randomly and independently positioned, approximately parallel tracks, the average center-to-center distance from one track to its closest neighboring track.

## 14. FLASH effect on the liver and spleen of mice-a transcriptional perspective.
Authors: Ma Y, Zhu X, Yang T, Lv J, Yan X, Yang G, Lin X, Cao Z
Journal: Medical physics (2026)
Identifiers: PMID 42904553; DOI 10.1002/mp.70720
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42904553/
Category: Radiobiology | Tags: Radiobiology, Clinical & Translational

Abstract: BACKGROUND: FLASH radiotherapy (FLASH-RT), delivered at an ultra-high dose rate (typically ≥ 40 Gy/s), has been shown to reduce normal tissue toxicity compared with conventional dose-rate radiotherapy (CONV-RT). However, the transcriptomic mechanisms underlying FLASH-mediated protection remain incompletely understood. PURPOSE: This study aimed to compare the biological effects of FLASH and CONV irradiation on mouse liver and spleen tissues and to identify molecular pathways associated with the FLASH effect from a transcriptional perspective. METHODS: C57BL/6J male mice received a single 12 Gy abdominal dose delivered at either 2 Gy/min (CONV) or 100 Gy/s (FLASH). Twelve days after irradiation, spleen and liver tissues were harvested for histological section analysis and RNA-seq. Inflammatory responses in the spleen and liver were assessed by hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E staining) and immunohistochemical quantification. A series of bioinformatic analyses, such as gene enrichment, protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis, and transcription factor prediction, were performed to explore the effect of FLASH irradiation. Subsequently, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), western blot analysis, and immunohistochemical quantification were used to verify the bioinformatic results.
