    Proton Bragg peak flash stereotactic radiosurgery for recurrent glioblastoma reirradiation.

    Ma Y, Wang Y, Zhang T, Zhao X, Selvaraj B, Cheng C et al. — Physics and imaging in radiation oncology (2026)

    Open access Radiobiology

    TL;DR. Stereotactic Radiosurgery with robotic image-guided systems is promising for recurrent glioblastoma (rGBM), but long delivery times may increase morbidity and reduce accuracy. Proton single-energy Bragg-peak FLASH-RT (SEBP-FLASH) may address these limits by delivering each field in <1 s, with potential for normal-tissue sparing through the FLASH effect.

    Summary — AI-generated, curator-reviewed

    This planning study re-optimized recurrent glioblastoma reirradiation cases originally treated with robotic image-guided stereotactic radiosurgery, using a single-energy Bragg-peak proton FLASH (SEBP-FLASH) technique with patient-specific apertures. Plan quality metrics (GTV coverage, conformity index, GI50/GI30, DVHs) were compared between SEBP-FLASH and the robotic technique, with SEBP-FLASH achieving a conformity index of 1.4 ± 0.2 versus 1.9 ± 0.3 for the robotic system (p=0.001) and comparable gradient indices (GI50 2.9 ± 0.5 vs 2.7 ± 0.2, p=0.085; GI30 6.2 ± 1.8 vs 5.5 ± 0.5, p=0.189). Using a dose threshold of 5 Gy and minimum monitor unit of 400, brain V40 Gy/s dose-rate coverage exceeded 80%. Each SEBP-FLASH field was delivered in under 1 second, with the study reporting feasibility and reduced on-table time relative to the robotic technique rather than any measured biological or clinical outcome.

    Abstract

    BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Stereotactic Radiosurgery with robotic image-guided systems is promising for recurrent glioblastoma (rGBM), but long delivery times may increase morbidity and reduce accuracy. Proton single-energy Bragg-peak FLASH-RT (SEBP-FLASH) may address these limits by delivering each field in <1 s, with potential for normal-tissue sparing through the FLASH effect. MATERIALS AND METHODS: The SEBP-FLASH technique was used to reoptimize treatment for rGBM patients treated with robotic image-guided systems. Patient-specific aperture devices were incorporated into the SEBP-FLASH technique for sharper penumbra. The gross tumor volume (GTV) coverage, conformity index (CI), dose gradient index (GI50 and GI30), and dose-volume histogram (DVHs), were evaluated. Additionally, ultra-high dose-rate ratios in critical organs-at-risk were assessed. RESULTS: SEBP-FLASH successfully met all clinical objectives. Both SEBP-FLASH and robotic techniques showed comparable performance, with GI50 and GI30 values of 2.9 ± 0.5 and 6.2 ± 1.8 for SEBP-FLASH, versus 2.7 ± 0.2 and 5.5 ± 0.5 for robotic (p-values at 0.085 and 0.189). SEBP-FLASH demonstrated superior conformality, achieving a CI of 1.4 ± 0.2 compared to CK's 1.9 ± 0.3 (p-value 0.001). All other metrics were similar across both techniques. For the brain, the FLASH dose rate coverage (V40 Gy/s) exceeded 80% under a 5 Gy dose threshold and minimum monitor unit of 400. CONCLUSIONS: This study evaluated the dose-volume characteristics of SEBP-FLASH and robotic image-guided systems and found broadly comparable dose distributions, suggesting that SEBP-FLASH may be a feasible option for rGBM reirradiation. SEBP-FLASH also showed potential advantages in conformity, normal tissue sparing, and reduced on-table time.

    Ahn SHH, Filosa DI, Lysakovski P, Brons S, Abdollahi A, Debus J et al. — Physics in medicine and biology (2026)

    Treatment Planning & Optimization Radiobiology Modeling & Mechanisms Physics & Dosimetry

    TL;DR. &#xD;Patient-specific 3D range modulators (RMs) allow rapid and conformal particle therapy with monoenergetic beams and may support motion management and ultra-high dose rate (UHDR) studies. For carbon ions, however, relative biological effectiveness (RBE) varies strongly with depth, so RM geometry should be optimized for biological effect rather than physical dose alone.

    Summary — AI-generated, curator-reviewed

    This study develops patient-specific 3D range modulators (bio-RMs) for carbon ion therapy that are optimized for RBE-weighted biological effect rather than physical dose, using the modified microdosimetric kinetic model and local effect model 1, with cell-line-specific mMKM parameters derived for a non-small cell lung cancer line. Bio-RMs designed for 4 and 8 Gy(RBE) biological spread-out Bragg peaks were 3D printed and validated with clonogenic assays, showing measured survival fractions within 5% of mMKM predictions and physical dose measurements within 1.2% of Monte Carlo calculations. Comparative treatment planning for vertebral metastasis and lung tumor cases showed bio-RM plans achieved target coverage and OAR metrics comparable to reference IMPT, with slightly reduced target homogeneity, and estimated delivery times reduced from minutes to seconds by eliminating energy switching. Robustness analyses indicated acceptable stability under manufacturing, setup, and range uncertainties evaluated. The work is framed as a basis for future motion-management and ultra-high-dose-rate studies at synchrotron-based facilities but does not itself report UHDR delivery or dose-rate-dependent biological effects.

    Abstract

    &#xD;Patient-specific 3D range modulators (RMs) allow rapid and conformal particle therapy with monoenergetic beams and may support motion management and ultra-high dose rate (UHDR) studies. For carbon ions, however, relative biological effectiveness (RBE) varies strongly with depth, so RM geometry should be optimized for biological effect rather than physical dose alone. This work presents a translational pathway for biologically optimized 3D RMs (bio-RMs) for carbon ion therapy, evaluated through dosimetric verification, radiobiological validation, and patient-specific planning. &#xD;&#xD;Approach.&#xD;A fast Monte Carlo-based RM platform was extended to perform RBE-weighted optimization using the modified microdosimetric kinetic model (mMKM) and local effect model 1 (LEM1). Cell line-specific mMKM parameters were determined for a non-small cell lung cancer cell line. Bio-RMs were designed for 4 and 8 Gy(RBE) biological spread-out Bragg peaks (SOBPs), 3D printed, and evaluated with clonogenic assays. Clinical feasibility was assessed through comparative treatment planning for vertebral metastasis and lung tumor cases.&#xD;&#xD;Main results.&#xD;Measured survival fractions agreed with mMKM predictions within 5% on average across the biological SOBP, and physical dose measurements agreed with Monte Carlo calculations within 1.2%. Secondary fragment analysis showed that the bio-RMs increased the relative contribution of light and intermediate fragments. In the retrospective clinical cases, bio-RM plans achieved target coverage and OAR metrics comparable to reference intensity modulated particle therapy (IMPT), with slightly reduced target homogeneity. Estimated delivery times were reduced from minutes to seconds by eliminating energy switching. Robustness analyses showed acceptable stability under the evaluated manufacturing, setup, and range uncertainties.&#xD;&#xD;Significance. &#xD;Bio-RMs enable fast, biologically conformal carbon ion therapy with plan quality approaching IMPT in the two cases studied. The framework links biological modeling to patient-specific implementation and provides a basis for future motion-management and UHDR studies in synchrotron-based facilities.&#xD.

    TL;DR. FLASH radiotherapy delivers ultra-high dose rate radiation (>40 Gy/s) has shown promise in reducing normal tissue toxicity while maintaining tumor control.     Summary — AI-generated, curator-reviewed

    This study reports commissioning and validation of a synchrocyclotron-based ultra-high-dose-rate pencil beam scanning proton beamline (IBA ProteusONE with S2C2, 228 MeV transmission beam) and its integration into the RayStation treatment planning system. The beamline achieved average dose rates exceeding 40 Gy/s for a 2.5 x 2.5 cm field, with nozzle current linear with requested MU across 45 to 126 nA. The TPS beam model, incorporating virtual source position, spot size, integrated depth dose, and absolute dose calibration, agreed with ionization chamber, film, and 2D scintillation detector measurements to within less than 2.5% deviation for point doses, and achieved gamma passing rates above 95% under 2%/2 mm criteria for 2D profiles including fields with brass apertures. The authors describe this as a framework for preclinical FLASH radiotherapy planning, with future work extending commissioning to all gantry angles and spread-out Bragg peak delivery.

    Abstract

    PURPOSE: FLASH radiotherapy delivers ultra-high dose rate radiation (>40 Gy/s) has shown promise in reducing normal tissue toxicity while maintaining tumor control. IBA's single-room proton system, equipped with S2C2 superconducting synchrocyclotron accelerator, has recently demonstrated to achieve UHDR delivery. Integrating the UHDR beam line in the treatment planning system (TPS) is crucial for accurate dose calculation in preclinical study, optimization of the 2D dose profile as well as paving the way for further accessory development for spread-out Bragg peak FLASH. This study aims to commission and validate a synchrocyclotron-based pencil beam scanning UHDR proton beamline on the IBA ProteusONE system in RayStation TPS. The goal is to establish a framework for TPS modeling and validation, facilitating preclinical FLASH radiotherapy studies. METHODS: The transmission UHDR beamline using scanning proton beam energy of 228 MeV was characterized at gantry 0° using comprehensive point dose and 2D lateral profiles measurements. The beam model was developed in RayStation, incorporating key parameters such as virtual source position, spot size, integrated depth dose (IDD), and absolute dose calibration. Extensive validations were conducted using ionization chambers, film dosimetry, and 2D scintillation detectors, with gamma analysis performed to assess the accuracy of the TPS model with open field and field in the presence of brass apertures. RESULTS: The UHDR beamline achieved ultra-high dose rates exceeding 40 Gy/s (average dose rate for a 2.5 x 2.5 cm field) with consistent dose output validated across multiple detectors. The nozzle current was measured to be linear with respect to the requested MU in the range of 45 to 126 nA. The RayStation beam model demonstrated excellent agreement with experimental measurements, achieving less than 2.5% deviation for all point dose measurements. For 2D profile measurements, gamma passing rates >95% under 2%/2 mm criteria for all fields. The TPS allowed optimization of the spot pattern for UHDR FLASH beams aligned closely with clinical beam profiles, enabling accurate preclinical study comparisons. CONCLUSIONS: A synchrocyclotron-based UHDR beamline was successfully commissioned and validated through a reliable TPS model for transmission FLASH application. The results provide a foundation for preclinical FLASH-RT research and future clinical applications, demonstrating the feasibility of integrating FLASH-RT into existing proton therapy platforms. Future work will extend the commissioning to all gantry angles and explore spread-out Bragg peak FLASH delivery for improved dose conformality.

    Summary — AI-generated, curator-reviewed

    This retrospective planning study modeled proton transmission FLASH radiotherapy for postmastectomy chest wall treatment (32 Gy in 5 fractions) in five left-sided breast cancer patients using single-energy (249 MeV) tangential transmission beams with a supplemental en face beam. FLASH effect was quantified using two dose-rate models, Krieger's FLASH effectiveness model (FEM) and Folkerts' average dose-rate (ADR) framework, applying a voxel threshold of at least 4 Gy at at least 40 Gy/s and a dose adjustment factor of 0.67. Increasing vertical scan speed from 10 to 20 mm/ms increased FLASH effect in the clinical target volume by 22% (ADR) and 12% (FEM), and in skin from 41.4% to 58.8% (ADR) and 8.4% to 13.1% (FEM). Split-field delivery and genetic-algorithm-optimized spot sequencing improved temporal separation between scan columns, yielding up to 9.2 Gy and 7.87 Gy reductions respectively in FLASH-corrected CTV mean dose under the ADR model, with skin mean dose reductions of 2 to 3 Gy.

    Abstract

    PURPOSE: We investigated the effect of scanning speed, beam configuration, and dose-rate modeling on the FLASH effect in postmastectomy proton transmission beams planning and evaluated the potential of spot scanning path optimization for enhancing the FLASH effect. METHODS AND MATERIALS: Five patients with left-sided postmastectomy breast cancer (32 Gy/5 fractions) were retrospectively replanned with single-energy (249 MeV) tangential transmission beams, supplemented by a clinical en face beam for dose homogenization. FLASH evaluation employed 2 models: Krieger's FLASH effectiveness model (FEM) and Folkerts' average dose-rate (ADR) framework. Plans were simulated under conventional pencil beam scanning, split-field, and optimized spot sequences (using genetic algorithm [GA]), with vertical scan speeds varied from 10 to 20 mm/ms. FLASH effect in normal tissues was quantified by the percentage of voxels meeting the threshold (≥4 Gy at ≥40 Gy/s). A dose adjustment factor of 0.67 was applied to voxels meeting FLASH criteria to compute FLASH-weighted dose metrics in normal tissues within the chest wall target, whereas the physical dose to tumor cells remained unchanged.
